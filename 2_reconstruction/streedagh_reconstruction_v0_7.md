**Hull Scale, Military Superstructure, Wrecking Process and Archaeological Predictions**

Working control model — Version 0.7, 7 July 2026

Purpose. This document constrains subsequent analysis and article revision. It records the present working model, separates evidence from inference, identifies earlier assumptions that should no longer be used, and defines tests capable of revising or rejecting the model.

# 1. Status and epistemic rules

The reconstruction is provisional. Historical measurements, administrative ratings, personnel counts, artillery data, archaeological observations and wreck-process hypotheses are retained as separate evidence channels. No single channel is permitted to silently determine the others.

OBSERVED / SOURCE = directly recorded historical, archaeological or measured information.  
DERIVED = arithmetic transformation of source values with the operation stated.  
INFERRED = reconstruction constrained by several observations but not directly observed.  
HYPOTHESIS = physically plausible explanatory model requiring further testing.

Control rules: do not equate historical esloria with modern LOA unless demonstrated; do not equate toneladas or toneles machos with modern metric tonnes, displacement, deadweight or gross tonnage; do not treat E × M × P as direct hold volume.

# 2. Why the earlier reconstruction is superseded

The earlier approach implicitly imagined the Streedagh ships as scaled versions of modern replicas and moved too quickly from historical tonnage to modern mass. That produced false precision and a geometrically misleading picture. The revised model recognises that broad-bodied merchant hulls could carry extensive, vertically stacked fore and aft superstructures, especially when adapted to transport large infantry contingents and military equipment.

# 4. Hull-form relationships

The broad-bodied ships show a stable esloria:manga relationship: Visón ≈ 2.97, Lavia ≈ 2.79 and Regazona ≈ 2.81. The Galeón del Duque is much more elongated at 4.00. This supports distinct form families and warns against using a galleon replica as a direct geometric analogue for the Streedagh merchant transports.

Provisional Juliana historical measured-body envelope: esloria approximately 29.5–33 m, beam approximately 10.6–11.7 m, depth approximately 6.1–6.7 m. These figures are INFERRED and must not be relabelled as modern external LOA × beam × depth.

# 5. Personnel as an inverse architectural constraint

Juliana carried approximately 65–70 mariners and 290–325 soldiers: total 355–395 persons. Lavia carried about 342 persons; Visón about 306.

Using the reported historical allocation of approximately 1.5–2.0 m² per soldier, Juliana's infantry alone implies 435–650 m² of allocated area. This does not represent comfort space and need not be exclusive or simultaneous, but it is a strong inverse constraint. The vessel also had to preserve circulation and working access for mariners while carrying artillery, stores and heavy invasion equipment.

# 6. Revised architectural hypothesis

Preferred working model: a large broad and deep merchant hull, retaining a broadly conventional carrying body, with extensive military-use superstructure rather than a radically enlarged hull.

Provisional architecture:  
• Two principal longitudinal area-bearing levels within / over the main body.  
• A substantial forecastle, plausibly with about two usable levels.  
• A larger aftercastle, plausibly with about two to three usable levels.  
• Additional upper platforms or partial decks where structurally feasible.  
• Functional overlap through time: troop accommodation, movement, combat and artillery service did not require every area to be exclusive at every moment.

# 7. Provisional castle-area envelopes

Minimum model: forecastle 40–60 m²; aftercastle 70–100 m²; other platforms 0–30 m²; total added 110–190 m².  
Workable model: forecastle 60–100 m²; aftercastle 120–180 m²; other platforms 20–60 m²; total added 200–340 m².  
Upper model: forecastle 100–150 m²; aftercastle 180–280 m²; other platforms 50–100 m²; total added 330–530 m².

Preferred provisional superstructure addition: approximately 200–390 m² of usable area, achieved through stacked partial levels rather than one flat deck. An illustrative arrangement is a two-level forecastle contributing roughly 80–120 m² and a two- to three-level aftercastle contributing roughly 120–270 m². These are HYPOTHESIS ranges, not measured dimensions.

# 8. Artillery constraint

Historical records give Juliana an original complement of 32 guns. Archaeologically, 12 guns have been recovered: 3 in the 1980s and 9 in 2015. The current detailed gun table covers the 9 guns recovered in 2015 and subjected to archaeological analysis. Those nine span approximately 185 kg to 992 kg per piece, with a combined estimated mass of roughly 4.8–5.1 metric tonnes. The mixed battery is architecturally important: light and heavy pieces need not occupy equivalent positions or impose equivalent deck requirements.

There is no 12-versus-32 accounting conflict. The 32-gun figure is the documented original complement; 12 is the archaeological recovery total to date. On the present evidence, 20 of the documented original guns remain archaeologically unresolved. 'Not recovered' must not be equated automatically with 'still present on site': possible pathways include burial, dispersal, historical salvage, undocumented recovery or other loss.

Architectural implication (HYPOTHESIS): a 32-gun mixed battery on a broad-bodied merchant transport should not be modelled automatically as one homogeneous gun deck. A testable distribution model places the heaviest pieces preferentially in structurally stronger and lower positions, medium pieces across principal fighting positions, and some lighter pieces in upper works, castles or specialised positions. This is a prediction to be tested against gun types, recovery locations and structural evidence.

Site-assessment implication: the difference between 32 documented guns and 12 recovered guns creates a potentially substantial unresolved heavy-material budget. Up to 20 documented guns are archaeologically unresolved on the current evidence; their status must remain open rather than being assumed.

# 9. Handling and vulnerability hypothesis

Large elevated fore and aft works would increase projected wind area and aerodynamic forcing above the waterline. Combined with deep loading, heavy equipment, hundreds of persons and possible trim changes, this could increase leeway and reduce handling margins in severe weather.

The safe formulation is not that the ships are proven to have been 'hard to steer', but that the proposed modifications plausibly increased windage and degraded manoeuvring margins, especially near a lee shore. This should later be tested using reconstructed projected areas, approximate centres of effort, draught / displacement envelopes and comparative sailing evidence.

# 10. Differential grounding and breakup model

Preferred process sequence:

anchor failure or dragging → shoreward translation → grounding on a shallow sandbank / bar / breaker zone → rapid deceleration and constraint of the heavy lower hull → continuing wind and wave forcing on tall upper structures → rapid failure of castles, upper decks, masts and connected framing.

The model does not require instantaneous destruction of the entire lower hull. A contemporary observer could accurately perceive catastrophic 'complete breakup' while substantial bottom structure remained coherent, later buried beneath mobile sand. This reconciles rapid witnessed disassembly with articulated archaeological structure.

# 11. Casualty mechanism

If large numbers of troops occupied elevated or enclosed superstructures, rapid structural failure provides a plausible mechanism for exceptional casualties: collapsing floors and framing, crushing, sudden immersion, blocked exits, entanglement in rigging and timber, and direct exposure to breaking surf. This remains a HYPOTHESIS and should not be presented as demonstrated fact.

# 12. Archaeological predictions

• A comparatively coherent lower-hull core may survive beneath or within mobile sand.  
• Dense ballast, heavy cargo and engineering / siege equipment should show shorter displacement than buoyant upper timbers.  
• Collapsed fore and aft works should generate broad peripheral structural and fastening fields.  
• Original vertical position may partly predict final horizontal dispersion.  
• Heavy artillery may preserve relatively local depositional loci; lighter pieces and upper fittings may be more dispersed.  
• The archaeological footprint should exceed any footprint derived naively from esloria.  
• A timber-dominated superstructure field may be poorly represented by magnetometer data.  
• Repeated exposure and reburial can produce intermittent stability while still allowing destructive burst events.  
• Rudders and other large structural elements can become mobile after long burial, so 'preserved in situ' must be evaluated dynamically rather than assumed from temporary burial.

# 18. Iconographically Refined Stepped-Castle Architecture

Status: HYPOTHESIS constrained by approximately contemporary ship iconography, personnel counts, known military function and the existing Juliana hull envelope.

The earlier shorthand of a two-level forecastle and two- to three-level aftercastle is retained but refined. The preferred model is now a stepped architecture rather than a stack of equal rectangular floor plates.

- Aftercastle: two lower upperwork levels may extend forward close to the waist; a third, shorter upper level may occupy only the after portion of the castle.

- Forecastle: approximately two principal raised levels are provisionally preferred, with an accessible roof or fighting platform.

- Upper roofs and setback terraces are to be treated as potentially accessible open surfaces where rails, bulwarks or other iconographic indications support routine occupation.

- The uppermost levels should not be assumed to have the same headroom as lower working decks.

# 19. Functional Vertical Gradient and Provisional Deck Heights

HYPOTHESIS: vertical spacing decreased upward because functional volume requirements changed with height. Lower spaces required clearance for cargo handling, artillery, stores, equipment and circulation; upper castle spaces could tolerate reduced headroom where sleeping, sitting, waiting and short-range movement predominated.

- Lower working / storage level: provisional 2.0–2.3 m.

- Gun / mixed operational level: provisional 1.9–2.1 m.

- First castle level above main deck: provisional 1.8–2.0 m.

- Second castle level: provisional 1.6–1.8 m.

- Short upper aftercastle level: provisional 1.4–1.7 m.

- Roof surfaces: open occupied area, subject to weather and operational constraints.

These are modelling parameters, not measured dimensions. Deck beams, camber, hull curvature and local structural intrusions must be represented separately when usable headroom is calculated.

# 20. Three-Class Area Model

Future capacity calculations must distinguish area by function rather than summing all horizontal surfaces indiscriminately:

- Enclosed or semi-enclosed occupation area: sleeping, protected waiting, personal equipment and other troop use.

- Open occupied area: castle roofs, setback terraces and platforms usable for circulation, mustering, daylight activity, combat and possibly fair-weather sleeping.

- Operational sailing area: waist, rig-handling routes, hatch access and other surfaces that must remain substantially available to mariners.

Open roof area is not equivalent to enclosed accommodation, but it can absorb daytime population and circulation, thereby reducing pressure on enclosed compartments. Weather-dependent compression should be modelled explicitly.

# 21. Personnel Capacity as a Geometric Test

Juliana's current working envelope remains approximately 29.5–33 m historical measured-body esloria, 10.6–11.7 m beam and 6.1–6.7 m depth, with approximately 65–70 mariners and 290–325 soldiers. The earlier 1.5–2.0 m² per-soldier allocation remains a comparison constraint, not a fixed truth.

The revised test is bottom-up: calculate hull and deck planforms; estimate tapered usable areas; add stepped forecastle and aftercastle floor plates; add accessible roof and terrace surfaces separately; subtract masts, stairs, hatches, guns, recoil zones, framing, stores and protected mariner circulation; then compare the remaining capacity with the documented personnel complement.

A key possible outcome is that the ship may provide more distributed human-use area than the simple 1.5 m²-per-soldier assumption suggests, especially when open upper surfaces and time-sharing of spaces are included. This must be calculated rather than presumed.

# 22. Combat-Readiness and Spatial-Organisation Hypothesis

The castles were fighting structures as well as accommodation volumes. Elevated positions allowed soldiers and light artillery to engage opposing decks from above during close action and boarding combat. This creates a functional reason for troops to occupy the same raised structures in which they would fight.

HYPOTHESIS: substantial troop occupation of forecastle and aftercastle spaces reduced the need to move large infantry bodies from deep below decks through narrow hatches and congested working routes when action began. Men, personal weapons and at least some fighting equipment could already be distributed near elevated combat stations. Light guns in upper works would fit the same spatial logic.

This does not yet demonstrate one company per castle or permanent assignment of specific units to particular levels. Those remain testable organisational hypotheses.

# 23. Revised Operational Interpretation

The preferred model no longer treats hundreds of soldiers as a passive mass packed either onto the main weather deck or indiscriminately below decks. Instead, the ship is modelled as a vertically zoned military transport in which:

- the waist and essential weather-deck routes retain priority for sailing operations;

- lower body spaces carry heavy stores, artillery, equipment and selected personnel functions;

- large fore and aft upperworks distribute troops vertically and longitudinally;

- open castle roofs and terraces provide weather-dependent circulation and occupation space;

- the same elevated structures can transition from accommodation/occupation to combat use.

## 26.7 Provisional interpretation

In the central geometry, the stepped castles generate approximately 415 m² gross enclosed castle floor and 181 m² gross open upper surface. After the explicit deductions used here, this becomes about 291 m² net enclosed and 104 m² net open upper area, or 394 m² combined upper human-use surface.

The result does not show that every soldier had a permanent private allocation. It does show that a stepped, occupied castle system can create a large distributed human-use environment without filling the main weather deck. In the central case, adding a limited share of one lower principal deck moves the model into or near the previously cited 1.5–2.0 m²-per-soldier range for much of the personnel envelope. The exact result is highly sensitive to castle length, taper and operational deductions, which is why the full low/central/high table is retained.

The emerging functional model is therefore coherent: mariners retain the waist and essential weather-deck routes; heavy stores and equipment occupy lower volume; some infantry can occupy a lower principal level; and large infantry populations are distributed through fore and aft upperworks whose roofs also provide fair-weather circulation and combat surfaces.

# 30. Historical Convergence: Levant Squadron Architecture and Tactical Intent

Status: major convergence between an independently developed bottom-up reconstruction and subsequently introduced historical testimony. The sequence of inference must be preserved. The broad, castle-rich, vertically zoned transport model was developed from personnel counts, hull proportions, area constraints, contemporary iconography, artillery heterogeneity and operational logic before the Bertendona testimony entered the analysis.

# 30.1 Independent reconstruction sequence

The reconstruction first inferred that a low-profile galleon-like vessel could not comfortably accommodate Juliana's approximately 290–325 soldiers while preserving a workable sailing deck. Corrected Castilian metrology and broad-bodied comparators then indicated a short, wide merchant form. Contemporary iconography independently suggested stepped fore and aft works: about two forecastle levels, two long aftercastle levels, a shorter upper aft level, and occupied roof or terrace surfaces. Functional analysis assigned mariners priority over the waist and essential working routes, while infantry were distributed through upperworks and selected lower principal-deck space. The mixed recovered artillery spectrum further favoured a vertically differentiated battery over a single homogeneous gun deck.

# 30.2 Bertendona testimony as an external historical constraint

WORKING HISTORICAL REPORT: Martín de Bertendona, Levant Squadron commander, described the ships in correspondence to Philip II using the language of 'grandeza', potentially carrying senses of greatness, magnitude, grandeur or magnificence. He reportedly acknowledged risks associated with these large vessels, particularly in Atlantic service, while regarding their scale and height as conferring a major tactical advantage in close-quarter fighting. The exact Spanish wording, date and archival or published citation must be verified before quotation. As a working constraint, the testimony independently matches the inferred properties: exceptional scale, high fighting works, close-action utility and an acknowledged handling or seaworthiness cost.

# 30.3 Hull construction and reinforcement hypothesis

WORKING HISTORICAL DESCRIPTION: relevant Mediterranean bulk transports could be extremely beamy, in some cases with beam approaching half keel length. They may have originated as comparatively economical bulk carriers, including grain transporters, rather than purpose-built Atlantic warships. The supplied historical description also reports high and broad hulls strengthened with substantial wales and riders, extensive castle works fore and aft, a three-masted rig and bowsprit. Preferred interpretation: not simply a 'flimsy ship', but a structurally economical commercial carrying body subjected to reinforcement and military adaptation. Exact scantlings remain to be established.

# 31. Ammunition and Military-Logistics Constraint

Working historical figures: La Lavia carried 1,250 shot, 39 quintales of powder, 15 lead and 12 match; La Juliana carried 1,600 shot, 67 quintales of powder, 12 lead and 12 match; Santa María de Visón carried 236 soldiers, 71 seamen, 18 guns, 900 shot, 32 quintales of powder, 12 lead and 10 match. Units are retained as recorded pending verification of the applicable quintal and associated standards. Visón is analytically separated because the supplied listing places her in a squadron of pataches and zabras rather than the Levant Squadron.

# 31.1 Spatial implication of ammunition burden

Juliana's reported 1,600 shot and 67 quintales of powder define a major ammunition-management problem independent of personnel capacity. Reserve powder, shot, lead and match required protected storage, controlled access and transfer routes. This strengthens the case against indiscriminate mass infantry accommodation in lower military-storage spaces. HYPOTHESIS: substantial infantry occupation of fore and aft upperworks created useful separation between the main troop population and deep powder/ammunition stores. This is operationally and safety-wise coherent, but no formal historical fire-safety doctrine is asserted without textual evidence.

# 32. Provisional Functional Reconstruction of Juliana

The preferred model is a vertically zoned military transport. Deep lower body spaces carried ballast, bulk provisions, dense invasion stores, siege or engineering equipment and heavy cargo. Protected military-storage zones held reserve powder, shot, lead and match, with controlled transfer routes. Lower or principal fighting levels provided the strongest positions for heavy artillery and mixed sailor/gunner operations. The main weather deck and waist remained a mariner-priority operational system for rig handling, hatch access, anchors, boats and movement. The forecastle comprised about two principal raised levels plus an accessible roof or fighting platform. The aftercastle comprised two long lower upperwork levels extending toward the waist, a shorter third level aft, setback terraces and an occupied roof, making it the principal candidate for large-scale infantry occupation.

# 32.1 Infantry accommodation model

Under the medium and high stepped-castle models, upperworks provide approximately 374 m² and 520 m² respectively of net human-use surface before adding troop-compatible portions of a lower principal deck. These values permit distributed infantry occupation rather than permanent crowding of the operational weather deck. Soldiers could sleep or wait in enclosed upper levels, move onto roofs and terraces in fair weather, maintain weapons and equipment, and muster near combat stations. Severe weather would compress the population into protected spaces. The model does not require one company per castle, but company structure may have mapped onto architectural zones, watches or subdivisions.

# 32.2 Combat-readiness interpretation

Accommodation and fighting roles are mutually reinforcing. Infantry already occupying raised structures would not need to be moved en masse from deep compartments through narrow hatches and mariner working routes when action began. Occupation could transition directly into muster and combat. This is especially coherent with the reported Bertendona valuation of height and scale in close-quarter battle.

# 32.3 Artillery interpretation

Juliana's documented 32-gun complement and recovered spectrum from approximately 185 kg to nearly one tonne are provisionally interpreted as a vertically differentiated fighting system. Heavy pieces are predicted preferentially in stronger lower positions; medium pieces across principal fighting levels; selected lighter pieces in elevated castle or specialised positions. Guns reported high in the wreck field are therefore a priority test rather than an anomaly to explain away.

# 33. Scope Control: Juliana, Lavia and Visón

The present convergence most directly strengthens reconstruction of Juliana and provisionally La Lavia as a second Levant Squadron vessel testable through the same corrected metrology, rating sequence, personnel and logistics constraints, and castle-rich architectural model. Santa María de Visón must be held apart analytically for now because the supplied historical listing places her in a different squadron. The Levant model must not be transferred automatically.

# 34. Updated Epistemic Assessment

The stepped-castle reconstruction is strengthened because its principal architectural and operational properties were inferred before the Bertendona testimony entered the analysis. The testimony therefore functions as external convergence rather than as the premise from which the model was built. This does not prove every floor, troop station or gun position. It substantially raises the credibility of the general reconstruction and identifies falsifiable tests. Priority documentary task: locate and transcribe the Bertendona letter to Philip II; preserve exact Spanish wording around 'grandeza', risk, Atlantic conditions, height and close-quarter advantage; establish date, archive or publication, editorial interventions and translation before direct quotation.
